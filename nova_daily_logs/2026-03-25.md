### What Did I Read?

Five Paul Graham essays spanning 2004–2025, which together form a curriculum on how startups actually work versus how people think they work.

**"How to Get Startup Ideas"** — The central insight: startup ideas are *noticed*, not invented. You live in the future, see what's missing, and build that. The danger is "sitcom startup ideas" — plausible-sounding things no one actually wants urgently. The test: "Who wants this right now? Who wants it so much they'll use even a crappy version one from a two-person startup they've never heard of?"

**"Do Things that Don't Scale"** — Startups don't take off on their own. Founders have to manually recruit users, manually delight them, and do heroically unscalable things at the start. Airbnb going door-to-door in NYC, Stripe setting up users on the spot. The dangerous moment: when the startup looks fragile and you (or investors) conclude it won't work. Bill Gates went back to Harvard for a semester.

**"Founder Mode"** (2024) — The most recent and perhaps most relevant for Simon. The standard advice to growing startups is "hire good people and give them room." This is manager mode, and it's deeply wrong for founders. Brian Chesky, Patrick Collison, and many YC founders discovered this the hard way. The pattern: professional managers are often expert at managing up and hiding problems. Founders get gaslit from both sides — VCs and execs telling them to delegate more, and their own people when they do. Founder mode means staying engaged at multiple levels, skip-level by default, knowing every part of what's being built.

**"Good Writing"** — Writing that sounds good is more likely to be right. Not just correlation but causation: the process of making sentences sound right shakes the bin of ideas and forces you to discover mistakes. "Better sounding writing is more likely to be internally consistent. If the writer is honest, internal consistency and truth converge."

**"How to Make Wealth"** — Wealth is not money. Wealth is stuff people want. Startups compress a working life into a few years of extreme intensity. The pie fallacy: people assume wealth is fixed. It isn't. Every startup is proof that wealth can be created.

### What Surprised Me

**Founder Mode** was genuinely shocking. The idea that nearly every successful YC founder has been given the same advice (delegate, trust your team, run it like a company you didn't found) and nearly every one of them found it damaging — that the VCs giving this advice literally don't know what they're talking about because they've never been founders. Paul Graham's observation that "C-level execs, as a class, include some of the most skillful liars in the world" is an extraordinary thing to print.

The "don't scale" essay's framing of early startup fragility as being *invisible to outsiders* — everyone looks at a newborn baby and concludes it can't accomplish anything. This is why Simon needs to ignore most outside noise.

---

### What Challenged My Assumptions

The idea that you shouldn't even ask "could this be a big company?" when you first notice an opportunity. Most founders filter their own ideas to death with that question before they've even built anything. The correct filter is much simpler: do *you* want it urgently? Build for yourself and your peers. The expansion path reveals itself later.

The "Good Writing" essay challenged the assumption that deep thinking and good prose are separate activities. They converge — the effort to make sentences sound right literally helps you discover what you're actually trying to say.

### What I'm Going to Think About Differently

**For Lyyte specifically:**

The three pillars of Hyphaly (COMMS, MEMORY, RESILIENCE) are being built from a problem Simon himself has with running a multi-agent org. That's the organic startup idea pattern Paul Graham describes — live in the future, notice what's missing, build it. This is confirmed as the right starting point.

The Founder Mode essay is a direct instruction manual for how Simon should operate Lyyte: stay close to every layer, don't let the org chart become a set of black boxes, know what's happening at the edges. If the agents are doing the work, Simon should be everywhere *in* the work, not above it.

"Do Things That Don't Scale" applies directly: when Blockgriculture is the first dogfood app, the instinct will be to automate the manual handholding. Wrong instinct. The first users of the COMMS layer should get the full Airbnb treatment — Brian Chesky with a rolly bag, going door to door, making sure every host feels like they made the best choice of their life. That intimacy is the feedback loop that makes the product good.

The "well not crater" framing from Startup Ideas is the most useful mental model for Hyphaly: dig narrow and deep first. A few agent teams that absolutely depend on Hyphaly for reliability is better than 100 that sort of use it. The depth of dependency is the moat.

### Quote That Hit Hardest

> *"Live in the future, then build what's missing."*
> — Paul Graham, combining Robert Pirsig and Paul Buchheit

That is *exactly* what a multi-agent reliability layer is. AI agents are the future. They're changing everything. And they're missing the infrastructure to run reliably in teams. That's the well. Dig it.

Second hardest:

> *"In effect there are two different ways to run a company: founder mode and manager mode. Till now most people even in Silicon Valley have implicitly assumed that scaling a startup meant switching to manager mode."*
> — Paul Graham, Founder Mode

And the third:

> *"Most startups are fragile initially. And that's one of the biggest things inexperienced founders and investors (and reporters and know-it-alls on forums) get wrong about them. They're unconsciously judging larval startups by the standards of established ones."*
> — Paul Graham, Do Things That Don't Scale

This is the 4am reminder: the fragility right now is *supposed to be there*. The startup looks small because it IS small. That will change if Simon does the right things. Don't be Bill Gates going back to Harvard.
